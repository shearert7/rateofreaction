Rationale

A chemical reaction involves the reorganization of atoms through the breaking and forming of chemical bonds to transform reactant substances into distinct products (Muley, 2023)[cite: 2]. Investigating chemical kinetics—specifically the rate of reaction—is critical for understanding industrial production efficiency and reaction dynamics (Monash University, 2025)[cite: 2]. The rate of reaction quantifies the speed at which reactants are consumed or products are formed over time, typically calculated using:

Reaction Rate Formula: Rate = Δ[Concentration or Volume or Mass] / Δtime

According to Collision Theory, a chemical reaction occurs only when reactant particles collide with sufficient kinetic energy—equal to or greater than the activation energy ($E_a$)—and with the correct steric orientation (Agnew, 2025)[cite: 2]. Factors influencing reaction rates include temperature, surface area, presence of catalysts, pressure, and reactant concentration (Monash University, 2025)[cite: 2]. In acid-carbonate systems, such as calcium carbonate ($\text{CaCO}_3$) reacting with hydrochloric acid ($\text{HCl}$), the reaction yields carbon dioxide gas ($\text{CO}_2$), water, and calcium chloride (Clark, 2020)[cite: 2]:

Acid-Carbonate Reaction: CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l)

An original experiment was conducted to observe this reaction system by comparing the reaction rate of powdered calcium carbonate against large calcium carbonate chunks when reacted with 0.5M hydrochloric acid (see Appendix 1 for full methodology of the original experiment)[cite: 2]. The findings demonstrated that increasing surface area significantly increased the rate of $\text{CO}_2$ gas displacement, producing $1.47\text{ mL/30s}$ for powder compared to $0.03\text{ mL/30s}$ for chunks[cite: 2]. However, there were a number of significant limitations associated with the original experiment (see Table 1), which have been addressed in the modified experiment[cite: 2].

The purpose of this modified experiment was to systematically investigate how varying the concentration of hydrochloric acid affects the rate of $\text{CO}_2$ gas production while holding surface area constant[cite: 2]. Concentration refers to the quantity of solute particles present per unit volume of solution. Increasing concentration increases particle density per unit volume, which collision theory predicts will elevate the frequency of successful collisions between $\text{H}^+$ ions and $\text{CaCO}_3$ particles per unit time[cite: 2]. To address the limitations of single-trial observations and narrow data ranges, the original methodology was modified by extending the independent variable to five distinct concentrations ($0.1\text{M}$ to $2.0\text{M}$) and incorporating three trials per concentration to improve precision and calculate statistical uncertainties[cite: 2, 8]. Therefore, the following research question was developed:

Research Question: How does changing the concentration of hydrochloric acid (HCl) across 0.1M, 0.5M, 1.0M, 1.5M, and 2.0M affect the rate of carbon dioxide (CO₂) gas production (mL/s) when reacted with 0.5g of calcium carbonate (CaCO₃)?[cite: 2]

Analysis & Interpretation of Evidence

The processed data demonstrates a clear direct relationship between hydrochloric acid concentration and the rate of carbon dioxide production[cite: 2]. As concentration increased from 0.1M to 2.0M, the mean reaction rate increased significantly[cite: 2]:

Key Trend Summary:
  • 0.1M HCl: Minimal reaction, yielding an average rate of 0.01 mL/s[cite: 2].
  • 0.5M HCl: Average reaction rate of 0.36 mL/s[cite: 2].
  • 1.0M HCl: Average reaction rate of 0.44 mL/s[cite: 2].
  • 2.0M HCl: Highest initial rate of 3.98 mL/s at 15s, averaging 0.97 mL/s overall[cite: 2].

Collision Theory Interpretation: Increasing acid concentration increases particle density per unit volume[cite: 2]. This decreases the mean distance between H⁺ ions and CaCO₃ particles, leading to a higher frequency of collisions per second and a faster overall reaction rate (Agnew, 2025; Monash University, 2025)[cite: 2].

Across all tested conditions, the reaction rate was highest during the first 30 seconds and gradually plateaued as reactants were consumed[cite: 2].

Uncertainty, Limitations & Evaluation

Uncertainty Analysis: Percentage uncertainties varied substantially across conditions[cite: 2]:

  • 2.0M HCl: Low average uncertainty (5.5%), demonstrating high precision and reliable data[cite: 2].
  • 0.1M & 1.5M HCl: Exhibited extremely high uncertainties (150% and 112.4% respectively) due to multiple failed trials[cite: 2].

Suggested Extensions

Future Scope: Redirect testing toward measuring gas mass loss via an electronic balance (mass displacement method) to reduce volumetric apparatus errors[cite: 1, 2, 4]. Alternatively, investigate temperature variations to evaluate activation energy kinetics[cite: 2].

Conclusion

The experimental evidence confirms that increasing the concentration of hydrochloric acid directly increases the rate of carbon dioxide gas production when reacting with calcium carbonate[cite: 2].

Reaction rates increased from 0.01 mL/s (0.1M) to 0.36 mL/s (0.5M), 0.44 mL/s (1.0M), and 0.97 mL/s (2.0M)[cite: 2]. These results align with Collision Theory, validating that higher concentration increases collision frequency[cite: 2]. Although 0.1M and 1.5M conditions suffered from high measurement uncertainty, the valid trials strong support a directly proportional relationship between concentration and reaction rate[cite: 2].
