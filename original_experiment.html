Mandatory Practical: Surface Area and Reaction Rate

Aim

Conduct an experiment to test the effect of increasing surface area on the rate of a reaction between calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) and hydrochloric acid (HCl).

Materials & Apparatus

  • Setup Apparatus: Stand and clamp, water trough, 100 mL measuring cylinder, 100 mL conical flask with stopper and plastic tubing.
  • Reactants: 0.5 g calcium carbonate powder, 0.5 g calcium carbonate chips, 25 mL of 0.5 M hydrochloric acid (HCl).
  • Timing & Measurement: Stopwatch and volumetric gas measuring setup.

Experimental Variables

Variable Type Description
Independent Variable Surface area of calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) — Powder vs. Chips (Rocks).
Dependent Variable Volume of CO₂ gas produced (mL) recorded every 30 seconds.
Controlled Variables Mass of CaCO₃ (0.5 g), volume of acid (25 mL), acid concentration (0.5 M), temperature, and atmospheric pressure.
Diagram 1: Experimental Setup (Gas Displacement Method)

Methodology

  1. Set up the conical flask, stopper, tubing, water trough, measuring cylinder, clamp, and stand according to Diagram 1.
  2. Weigh out 0.5 g of calcium carbonate powder and place it into the conical flask.
  3. Measure out 25 mL of 0.5 M hydrochloric acid and set it aside.
  4. Prepare a stopwatch and assign a designated reader to observe the measuring cylinder volume every 30 seconds.
  5. Pour the prepared acid into the conical flask and immediately seal it securely with the rubber stopper.
  6. Record the volume of CO₂ gas produced at 30-second intervals.
  7. Stop recording when the gas volume remains unchanged for more than 2 minutes, or when 5 minutes (300 s) have elapsed.
  8. Empty, rinse, and clean the conical flask.
  9. Repeat the procedure using 0.5 g of calcium carbonate chips (rocks).

Limitations vs. Uncertainty

Limitations are flaws or constraints in the experimental design or methodology itself (e.g., small sample size, single trial, uncontrolled variables).
Uncertainty is the quantifiable doubt in a measured numerical value based on instrument resolution (e.g., ±0.05 g or ±0.5 mL).

Background

When evaluating experimental methodologies, modifications are designed to systematically address identified limitations, minimize measurement uncertainties, or expand scientific scope. Modifications are categorized into three distinct operational pathways:

Modification Primary Objective Impact on Quality What Changes vs. Preserved
Refinement Fix procedural flaws & lower measurement uncertainties Increases Precision, Accuracy, & Reliability Changes: Equipment resolution, trial count, timing frequency.
Preserves: Research question & variables.
Redirection Shift focus to a different kinetic factor when current setup fails or yields unexpected trends Restructures Experimental Scope & Validity Changes: Independent variable (e.g., Temp or Molarity).
Preserves: General reaction system.
Extension Expand range or granularity of the independent variable to establish mathematical relationships Enhances Validity & Data Range Resolution Changes: Number of test levels (e.g., 2 levels → 5 levels).
Preserves: Core methodology & reaction type.

Note on Scientific Rigor: A well-justified modification plan must combine at least one structural path change (Redirection or Extension) with multiple targeted Refinements to guarantee that collected data is both highly valid and statistically reliable.
